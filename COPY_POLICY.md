# Public-facing copy policy

Applies to website and application text, documentation, product descriptions,
and other public-facing prose. Follow a specific creative brief for fiction,
dialogue, or deliberately stylized campaigns; do not impose neutral interface
language on those forms.

## Establish meaning before wording

Before drafting, inspect the actual content, implemented behavior, and approved
project facts. Identify the reader's question or intended action and the facts
needed to answer it. Do not treat existing AI-generated copy as verified facts.

Do not invent features, source material, audiences, quantities, credentials,
benefits, licensing terms, or editorial practices. When essential facts are
missing, report the gap rather than disguising it with vague language.

## Match the text to its job

Interface labels: name the destination, content, or action in familiar terms.
Use "Library" for a library, not a slogan in place of a recognizable label.
Button text must describe what the button actually does.

Descriptions and documentation: explain the subject, behavior, or next step.
Use complete, natural sentences where explanation is needed.

Marketing: explain a specific offering, a relevant reader benefit, and supported
differences. Do not substitute excitement or self-praise for information.

Use an approved project voice and examples where available. Otherwise, use
ordinary, direct adult language. Do not force informality, cleverness, corporate
formality, or extreme brevity. Keep technical terms when they improve precision.

## Do not write text just to fill a component

A page does not automatically need an eyebrow, slogan, subtitle, introduction,
and closing call to action. Omit optional text that adds no useful information.
A title followed by the content may be sufficient. Make the component support
that instead of inventing filler to satisfy its layout.

Do not praise the writing's own clarity, readability, or lack of jargon.
Describe what the reader will find or learn. State a reading level or adaptation
method only when verified and useful.

Explain production methods when they matter to interpretation, trust, or required
disclosure. Keep source attribution and other important disclosures; do not delete
them merely to make copy shorter. Place details where readers need them.

## Reject these patterns

- Opaque, affected headings: "Written up" or "Ideas, unpacked" when the section
  simply needs a recognizable name.
- Empty benefit claims: "rewritten for reading," "built for the curious," or
  "knowledge, reimagined" without a concrete meaning relevant to the page.
- Stock promotional formulas used as decoration: "where X meets Y," "not just X,
  but Y," and strings of grand claims without evidence.
- Awkward circumlocutions and changing terminology: do not alternate between
  books, courses, articles, and publications as though they mean the same thing.
- Redundant introductions, unsupported superlatives, and invented distinctions.

These are contextual rejection criteria, not a universal forbidden-word list.
Do not replace a flagged phrase with an equally vague synonym.

## Reference examples

Context: a page lists publications; no additional explanatory facts are verified.
Reject: "Written up"
Prefer: "Library"
An introductory paragraph is optional, not required.

Context: articles really are adapted from university course materials and include
source references.
Reject: "Science and technology, taken from open university courseware and
rewritten for reading. Every piece names the book it came from."
Possible description: "Articles adapted from university course materials, with
references to the original sources."
Use this description only if it accurately describes the collection and helps
readers understand it. Do not copy its factual claims into unrelated projects.

## Preserve approved copy

During layout changes, refactoring, or feature work, preserve approved wording
unless the task requires a text change or changed behavior makes it inaccurate.
Do not paraphrase text merely to make the page feel fresh. Treat intentional copy
changes as reviewable changes, not incidental styling decisions.

## Review before completing the task

Review changed copy separately from code, in its page context. Check that readers
can identify the content or action, that each claim is supported, and that useful
meaning survives editing. Remove optional sentences whose deletion loses nothing
important. Read the wording for natural phrasing and consistent terminology.

When rejecting a draft, quote the specific problem and identify the defect:
unclear label, empty claim, unsupported fact, irrelevant process detail, redundancy,
awkward phrasing, or mismatch with the approved voice. Revise against that defect.
Do not use "sounds human" or an arbitrary quality score as the acceptance test.
